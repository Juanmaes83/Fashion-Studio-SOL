Hermes — The On-Model Reel Bot

Put a clothing store's real clothes on a model in a cinematic reel — and mail the owner the finished film.

Most offline clothing stores have great clothes and almost no content, because a real shoot means a model, a photographer, a studio, and a stylist. Hermes builds the thing first: a cinematic reel of their own clothes on a real model, landing in their mailbox on a postcard with a QR to watch. You're not selling an idea — you're handing them the finished result and letting it sell itself.

The big idea

The owner scans the code, watches their own dress walking through a sunlit garden, and the only question left is "how do I get more of these."

Instead of cold messaging a store and asking for a meeting, Hermes flips the pitch and builds the thing first — a cinematic reel of their own clothes on a real model.

That's a warm lead that closed itself before you ever spoke.

The timing

§

Why this works now

  • The intent is everywhere and unclaimed. Every town has clothing stores with weak visuals. Nobody is systematically turning their racks into model reels.
  • The assets are already public. Their clothes are on their website or in their Google listing photos. Everything you need is one scrape away.
  • Generative video changed the math. Putting a specific garment on a believable model in a believable location used to need a production. Now it's a prompt — a custom per-store reel is finally cheap enough to make on spec and mail cold.
  • A reel is the format that already wins. It's what the store would post anyway. You're giving them the exact thing their feed is missing.

Sourcing

01

Find the stores

Use the Google Places API to pull clothing stores in any city. Text Search and Nearby Search both work — query by type and keyword: clothing store, women's clothing store, boutique, dress shop, stacked with a city or a lat/long radius so you sweep metro by metro.

Per place you get name, website, address, rating, review count, and listing photos. Save the place id so you never process the same store twice. Rotate the city and the keyword for an endless, fresh stream.

Places finds the store. Scrapers go get the goods. Apify actors go deeper than the API — a store's full Shopify catalog or its Instagram grid, once Places hands you the website.
02

Qualify — keep the signal, drop the noise

Not every store is worth a reel. Filter for the ones where good video obviously moves product:

  • a real store with real inventory, not a dead listing
  • clothes that read well on a model — dresses, sets, statement pieces
  • reachable, so you can mail them or find a contact

A vision model is perfect here — feed it the listing photos and the site, let it grade fit and likely interest, then keep or skip automatically.

03

Get their clothing

Two paths — Hermes picks based on what the store has:

They have a website

Most boutiques run on Shopify, which quietly exposes a clean product feed. Hitting /products.json returns every product with its catalog images, usually shot on white or on a model — the cleanest possible input. Scrape it, skip anything that isn't clothing (bags, jewelry, hats), and you have their whole rack as images.

http · shopify-feed
GET https://{store-domain}/products.json?limit=250
# → products[].variants, products[].images[].src — their whole catalog

They don't have a usable site

Fall back to the Google listing photos — stores and shoppers post shots of the racks and the window. Pull those and extract the clothing out of them. Either way the goal is the same: clean images of the actual garments this store sells.

04

Pick the best piece

You don't reel the whole catalog. You pick one hero piece — the thing most likely to stop a scroll and most representative of the brand. A vision model ranks the candidates on silhouette, color, and "would a person screenshot this."

One store, one standout look. That's what becomes the reel.

The prep

05

Extract the garment

Catalog and listing photos are messy — a model's face, a busy store, a dress form, a phone in a mirror. Before you can put the garment on a fresh model cleanly, isolate it with ChatGPT Image Generation (Image Gen 2) as an edit step. Pass the product photo and lift out just the garment:

Garment isolation · Image Gen 2Isolate the single garment only. Remove the model, face, hands, mannequin, and the entire background. Present it as a clean ghost mannequin product shot, front facing, the whole piece centered on pure seamless white, soft even studio light. Preserve the exact color, print, fabric, neckline, straps, and hem. Photoreal. No text, no logo, no extra objects.

Now you have a pristine, isolated garment — the perfect controllable input for the next step, and it doubles as the "before" you can show alongside the finished reel.

The transformation

06

Put it on a model, render the reel

This is the moment. Take the clean garment and generate a cinematic video of it worn by a real-looking model, in a real location, shot like an ad. Use Seedance 2.0, passing the isolated garment as reference:

Cinematic reel · Seedance 2.0Vertical 9:16 cinematic fashion ad, about 12 seconds, no audio. A single model wears the exact dress from the reference, preserving its color, print, and cut. Bright location that contrasts with the dress so it pops. Multiple angles cut together like a real ad: a wide establishing shot, a walk toward camera, a slow turn as the fabric moves, a low angle hero shot, and a tight detail of the fabric. Bright, refined, premium. Keep the top and bottom of the frame clear. No text, no logos, no other people.
⌖ Two craft notes that make or break it

Pin the garment. Tell the model to preserve the exact dress from the reference — its color, print, cut, and length. The whole pitch dies if the garment drifts.

Strip the audio and keep it tight. A clean, short, muted vertical clip is exactly what drops into a feed. Lock the same model across a store's looks so the set feels like one shoot, and tune the location per store.

07

Host it and make a QR

Drop the reel somewhere it can be watched instantly at a URL — one page per store. Generate a QR that points to it. That QR is what turns a paper postcard into a click. Scribeless generates the tracked QR for you in the next step from this page URL.

Outreach — the knock on the door

08

Mail the postcard with Scribeless

Hermes composes a postcard — a striking frame from the reel on the front, the store's name, a short line, and the QR to watch — then mails it to the owner by name. The store gets a real piece of mail with their own clothes already turned into a film. Here's the exact build.

1 · Compose the card, render to print spec

Pull a striking frame from the reel, compose the front (frame + store name + one line), and normalize to 1875 × 1275 (4×6 @ 300 dpi). Optionally run the isolated garment "before" small alongside the reel still so the transformation reads at a glance.

ts · render-card.ts
import sharp from "sharp";

// heroFrame = a striking still pulled from the rendered reel
const frontPng = (await sharp(heroFrame)
  .resize(1875, 1275, { fit: "cover" })
  .png().toBuffer()
).toString("base64");

2 · Push the recipient to Scribeless

The endpoint is POST /api/recipients on platform.scribeless.co, authed with an X-API-Key header (not Bearer). The frame goes base64-inlined as an <img> inside variables.html; pass the reel page URL in the qrCode field and Scribeless generates + scan-tracks the code. Address it to the store at the Places address — to the owner by name when you have it. The campaignId is your studio's campaign — the sender brand.

bash · scribeless.sh
curl -X POST https://platform.scribeless.co/api/recipients \
  -H "X-API-Key: $SCRIBELESS_KEY" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
    "campaignId": "{{studioCampaignId}}",
    "data": [
      {
        "firstName": "{{ownerFirstName}}",
        "company": "{{storeName}}",
        "address": {
          "line1": "{{storeAddress}}",
          "city": "{{city}}",
          "state": "{{state}}",
          "postalCode": "{{zip}}",
          "country": "US"
        },
        "variables": {
          "html": {
            "front": "<img src=\"data:image/png;base64,{{frontPng}}\" />"
          },
          "qrCode": "https://yourstudio.com/reel/{{placeIdSlug}}"
        }
      }
    ]
  }'

Field-name gotchas: postalCode (not postal_code), the address is a nested object, and it's the store's public Places address — no records lookup. If Places only gave you the store name, address to company and drop firstName; pull the owner name from the site's About page when you want it on the card.

The warm lead loop

The QR is the signal. When the owner scans it, you know they watched — and that scan is your cue to follow up, or to let an automated reply do it. You're not chasing strangers. You're talking to owners who just watched their own store look incredible.

Making it autonomous

yaml · hermes-loop
1  Places sweeps on a schedule   → new stores into a queue
2  each store qualified          → kept or dropped automatically
3  kept stores scraped           → hero piece chosen
4  garment isolated              → rendered into a reel
5  reel hosted + QR generated    → postcard composed
6  Scribeless mails it           → scan webhook flags the warm leads

Memory is a database of processed place ids, so the loop never repeats a store. Set it running and it works the whole funnel — from a map pin to a mailbox — on its own. You just review the output and keep the bar high.
